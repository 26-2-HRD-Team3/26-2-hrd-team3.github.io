What AI changes

AI compiles the numbers; the marketer must interpret, verify and decide

Key metrics now come from one prompt, but a person still reviews and completes the analysis (Norway Q3). Weak input to AI produces weak output (Myanmar Q2).

Design principle: AI gathers the data; the marketer makes the strategic judgment and owns the final decision.

Our approach

Understand → Verify → Decide → Prove

Verifying an AI analysis against source data and campaign context (Area 4) needs the context-reading skill from Area 1 first. Choosing between promotional options (Area 3) then uses both: interpreted and verified evidence. Guidance decreases as Lily moves from modeled to supported to independent work.

Q1 · M1 · Assessment

Entry Assessment — where does Lily start?

The year opens with an assessment of all three areas, so the starting level is measured rather than guessed.

1 · Self-assessment

Lily reads the Developing and Proficient indicators for each area and marks where she stands now.

2 · Three short cases

One work sample per area: interpret a performance sheet (35 min), verify a short AI analysis (40 min), recommend one of four candidates (30 min). No AI, no personal job aids.

3 · Feedback interview

An alumni assessor who will not coach her scores the cases, then a 15-minute interview compares her self-view with the result and sets one-year goals.

AI: Claude drafts the cases; the assessor reviews every one. Learners use no AI.

Activity 1 When Numbers Whisper

Understanding Metrics in Context · A. Formal education (blended) · No AI

“Lily sees an engagement rate of 3%. Good or bad?”

It depends — on the goal, the platform, the format and the audience. (illustrative)

What Lily does. Three 60-minute recorded lectures, each with a 10-question quiz: what common metrics measure (M1), reading them by campaign goal (M2), and by platform, format and audience (M3).

Why this method. Concepts are taught by lecture; quizzes are retrieval practice. The game is limited practice on one context at a time, and the debrief turns it into principles.

Builds the Developing indicator — “Explains what common metrics measure and links them to the campaign objective.” — plus the four contexts of the Proficient indicator: goal, platform, format, audience.

M3 on-campus finale game · “Beyond the Numbers”
1Reveal a metric card
2Draw a random context card
3Explain how to read the metric
4The other team: “Go Beyond!”
5Peer referee rules, then feedback

Evidence: M3 quiz (8 of 10 or more) and individual game records — each learner’s explanation accepted at least twice. Output: a personal context checklist written in the debrief.

Activity 2 Connect the Dots

Finding the Context Behind the Metrics · D. Interpersonal relationships (mentoring or coaching) · Claude only prepares coach-reviewed data

Activity 1 focuses on interpreting individual contextual factors, while Activity 2 focuses on integrating multiple factors to analyze overall campaign performance.

M4Coach demo + structured form
M5Question-only form + peer review
M6Alone, no form

What Lily does. A one-page interpretation of a full campaign dataset each month, with written coach feedback and a 30-minute group coaching call. Support fades from M4 to M6.

Why this method. Practice with feedback and a model to imitate. Contexts interact, so learners practice the whole task; a new campaign each month builds transfer for an open skill.

◆ Korea may use data from an account the learner runs (coach-checked)
◇ Norway one dataset is a GA4 report

(2) At least one case where two or more contexts together change one metric’s reading
(3) No judgment based only on numbers going up or down

Activity 3 Line by Line

AI Report Verification & Revision Project · C. Job experiences (on-the-job experience) · Claude writes the reports learners verify

“An AI report says the campaign clearly worked. Would Lily share it?”

Not before checking it against the data and the campaign context.

M7 · Learn to verifyOnline modules + answer-keyed practice report
M8 · With supportPartner topic; verify every claim; practitioner feedback
M9 · AloneVerify first, then compare with an independent assessor

What Lily does. Pulls out each claim in a Claude report built from public data and records whether it is supported, differs from data or cannot be verified, and whether context is missing, with the source — then revises the report before sharing it.

Why this method. For a job seeker, on-the-job training means a real task with a real reviewer. Support is reduced step by step, and learners study what they missed in M8 before M9 (error-management training).

▲ Myanmar present and defend the verified report, in addition to the verification itself

Output · M9 success criteria

Portfolio 2: M9 verified report

(1) A verdict and evidence recorded for every claim
(2) 80%+ claim-level agreement with an independent judge
(3) No major problem left unresolved in the final report

Public data only — partner companies give a topic and feedback, never internal data.

Activity 4 Make Your Case

Social Media Marketing Decision Simulation · A. Formal education + AI-supported practice: Claude mock client (M10)

“Four influencers, one budget. Would Lily pick the biggest account?”

Not before comparing them against the campaign goal and target audience. (illustrative)

Three agencies compete to recommend one influencer to TikkiTakka Skin, a fictional skincare client. Below you can walk through the M10 day as a learner: read the brief, compare four candidates, rehearse with the client, handle a brief update and write your own recommendation.

Learning objective · Area 3 Proficient. Given a fictional client brief, four influencer profiles and a five-criteria comparison sheet; after the brief changes, working alone, before team discussion and without AI, each learner compares influencer options against the campaign goal and target audience using multiple criteria, recommends one option, and explains the trade-offs — within 20 minutes.

Recommendation ①

“Choose Mia. She has over seven times more followers and much higher reach.”

Recommendation ②

“Choose Theo. Our goal is to get university students to save planner tips, so I prioritized audience fit and saves in context. About 70% of Theo’s followers are university students, compared with about 30% of Mia’s, and his study-vlog content fits a study planner. His past Reels had a 5% save rate, compared with Mia’s 1%. I also checked sponsorship history and cost: Theo posts sponsored content less often, though that alone doesn’t prove better results, and charges $400 versus Mia’s $950, both within budget. Trade-off: Mia’s past Reels averaged more total saves (about 500 vs 400) and far greater reach, and we can’t tell how many of either creator’s saves came from students. I accept Theo’s lower observed total saves and reach because his followers and content are more closely aligned with our target. If our goal were broad awareness, I would give Mia’s reach more weight.”

Which recommendation is more convincing for this campaign, and why?

“Recommendation ② is more convincing not because it chose Theo, but because it starts from the goal and target, compares several criteria, admits Mia’s strengths, and explains why it still chose Theo. A recommendation for Mia could also be convincing if it were reasoned this way.”
In the program, teams then practice on a separate café case and rewrite one trade-off sentence after feedback.
